HEVN separates two completely different kinds of authorization:

| | Platform authorization | Money authorization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credential | HEVN JWT | Signature from a wallet owner key, or from a [remote transfer key](#remote-transfer-keys) within its onchain spend allowance |
| Grants | Contacts, invoices, contracts, virtual account details, KYC, statements, analytics | Transfers and any onchain state change |
| Issued by | HEVN backend | The signer's own key, per operation |
| If leaked | Metadata exposure; **funds untouched** | Owner key is never exposed (see [Privy](/general/privy-wallets)); a leaked remote transfer key is capped by its onchain allowance and revocable |

## Platform login flow

HEVN authenticates users the way web3 apps do — by proof of key ownership, not passwords:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sign in with Privy">
    The user authenticates with their email or Google account, unlocking their [Privy embedded wallet](/general/privy-wallets) in the session.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sign a login message">
    The app asks the Privy wallet to sign a challenge message. Signing a message is free, off-chain, and moves nothing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="HEVN verifies the signature">
    The backend recovers the signer address from the signature and checks it against the expected Privy address for that identity.
  </Step>
  <Step title="HEVN issues its own JWT">
    On success, HEVN issues a short-lived JWT scoped to platform features only.
  </Step>
</Steps>

There is no password database to breach and nothing HEVN stores that could be replayed to impersonate a wallet.

## What the JWT can and cannot do

The JWT authorizes the HEVN API surface: creating contacts, issuing and paying *requests* for invoices, generating contracts, requesting virtual account details, reading balances and statements.

<Warning>
  The JWT is **not** a spending credential. Even a fully compromised JWT cannot produce a wallet signature, and the wallet contract only accepts operations signed by an [onchain owner](/general/smart-wallets#ownership-verifiable-onchain). Money movement always terminates in a signature from an authorized key — the owner's, a teammate's, or a remote transfer key acting within its allowance.
</Warning>

## Remote transfer keys

To let a program pay from your account, you create a [remote transfer key](/remote-transfers/overview)
in **Settings → Remote Transfers**. It is not a JWT and it is not a wallet owner. The
[whitelabel REST API](/api-reference/authentication) uses developer keys and JWT sessions instead,
and rejects `X-Api-Key`.

Each key has a spender wallet of its own: a smart wallet owned by a signer HEVN derives from the key.
Its spending ability is governed onchain, by the same standard as [teammate access](/general/shared-access):
you sign a `SpendPermission` from your wallet naming that spender, with an explicit allowance and period.

The consequences of this design:

- **Without a spend permission, a key cannot move a single cent.** A transfer is a user operation from
  the spender wallet, and the `SpendPermissionManager` contract accepts it only within the granted
  allowance.
- **A leaked key is capped and revocable.** The worst case is bounded by the remaining onchain
  allowance for the current period. Deleting the key in the app revokes the permission onchain and
  kills the key in one action.
- **Your own key is never involved.** No key material leaves the [Privy layer](/general/privy-wallets);
  the remote transfer key is a separate identity with its own, strictly smaller, rights.

Remote transfers are for advanced use. For everyday payments, use the app or the HEVN assistant, which
only files transfer requests that you approve. Treat keys as secrets: one per integration, the
smallest allowance that works, stored outside your codebase.
